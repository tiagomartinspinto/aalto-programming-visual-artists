Projects

Three checkpoints turn weekly sketches into your own work: two short briefs, then one final project. Each asks for less prescription and more of your own direction than the one before it.

How These Work

Every project here is expected to start from something you already have, not a blank file. Open one or more exercises from the relevant sessions, and change them: modify a value, combine two ideas, remove the parts that don't matter to you, or rebuild only the piece your idea actually needs.

Save a few versions along the way. Two or three saved variations that show a different choice each time are enough to demonstrate a direction was found, not stumbled into - this matters more than any single version being polished.

Whatever you start from, you are expected to understand and intentionally change it. Copying code you can't explain is not the goal, even when it runs. Briefly note what you started from, what you changed, and what is now yours - a sentence or two is enough, not a citation report.

Project Brief 1 - Interactive Visual Composition

After Sessions 01-02, extendable to after Session 03.

Make something that responds to the viewer. A shape, a small scene, a composition - the subject is yours. What matters is that it visibly changes because someone is there: moving the mouse, hovering, or clicking.

You do not need every technique from these sessions. A strong project is entirely possible with mouse input, variables, and one function - or with a condition and simple movement. Choose whichever combination serves your idea, not the longest list.

Reasonable starting points: Face Exercise, Color House, Mouse Shapes, Hover Color, Bouncing Ball Color.

Before you call it done, ask: what single value does the viewer actually change, and how? Does the result read as one considered idea, or several unrelated effects stacked together?

Project Brief 2 - Generative Visual System

After Sessions 04-05, extendable to after Session 06.

Project Brief 1 was about response - one viewer, one interaction. This one is about a rule: design a system that can produce a family of related results, not one fixed picture.

A strong project is entirely possible with loops, one function, and random(). noise() and recursion are available, not required. If you understand loops, functions, and randomness well and use them with intention, that is a complete project - reaching for noise() or writing a recursive function does not automatically make a stronger one.

Reasonable starting points: Loop Row, Hover Grid, Simple Waves, Shapes Function, Noise Grid, Recursive Tree.

Before you call it done, ask: what is the one rule your system follows, in one sentence? What changes between results, and what stays the same? Would someone recognize a family resemblance across several runs?

Final Project

After Session 07.

Make one focused piece of interactive or generative work that reflects a visual direction that is actually yours. This is where the two briefs, and whatever from Sessions 01-07 genuinely serves your idea, come together - not a checklist of everything the course covered.

Complexity is not the goal. A final project built carefully from Sessions 1-4 ideas - interaction, variables, one well-designed function, a considered composition - can be just as strong as one that also uses generative systems, noise, recursion, or particles. What matters is intentional use and real understanding of the code you submit, not how much of the course you fit in. A recursive particle system does not automatically outrank a well-made interactive drawing.

Keep it small and finished. Aim for one small, focused piece you can actually finish and present - not a large application, a game, or a multi-part installation. A project that does one thing with real intention is stronger than several unfinished ones. If an idea keeps growing, that's usually a sign to cut it back to its most essential rule and build that properly.

Every exercise from Sessions 01-07 is fair starting material. Adapt, combine, strip down, restructure - whatever gets you to your own direction fastest.

Be ready to explain your code. You should be able to explain what the important parts of it do, point to what you changed or adapted from a starting example, and say how you'd change one part of it if asked. This isn't a technical exam - it means the code should be genuinely yours to walk through, however you arrived at it.

Before you call it done, ask: what is the one idea this project is actually about? Could you explain every important line if asked? Is this finished enough to show, even if it's small?

How This Is Assessed

These principles apply across both briefs and the final project. There are no fixed percentages here - your teacher will explain how they apply to grading for this specific course run.

Experimentation and process. Credit goes to trying alternatives, iterating, debugging, and discarding ideas that didn't work - not only to the final polished result. A couple of meaningfully different saved versions says more than one lucky first attempt.

Technical understanding. Can you explain what values are changing, what rule controls the behavior, and what your loops, functions, or objects are doing where you used them? This is judged against the techniques you actually chose, not against the most advanced material in the course - a mouse-and-one-function project is judged as exactly that, not marked down for skipping recursion.

Intentional use of code. The question is always whether the code serves your visual or artistic idea, not whether it demonstrates an advanced feature. Simple code used well is worth more than complex code used for its own sake.

Visual and artistic development. Your choices - composition, color, movement, rules, interaction - should show a personal direction, not just a working demo. This is a programming course for visual artists: the visual decisions matter as much as the code producing them.

Explaining and modifying your own code. Expect a short conversation about your work: what a section does, what you changed the most, what happens if one value changes, what you had to debug. This isn't an oral exam - it's how understanding becomes visible, however you arrived at the code.

After Session 07: Project Studio

The seven sessions cover the course's core techniques. Class time after Session 07 is intended for project studio work rather than a new programming topic: development time, debugging help, and critique on Project Brief 2 and the Final Project. There is no Session 08 in the core curriculum.
